Comparative style study · 12 source PDFs

What the machine-learning textbooks teach about writing these agent notes

A source-grounded style guide drawn from ten textbooks or monographs, one set of theory lecture notes, and one short algorithm paper. The aim is to combine their teaching strengths without flattening their different purposes into a single voice.

Prepared 12 September 2026 · Page references below use the PDF viewer's page count, not the number printed on the page.

How the references were skimmed

The comparison used 12 supplied PDFs. I inspected each file's front matter or introduction, its contents or section map, representative technical pages, and, where present, examples, exercises, labs, notation guides, or chapter notes. I also inspected rendered pages from six books to check how figures, equations, headings, and running text share the page. This is a style sample, not a cover-to-cover assessment of the books' technical claims.

Evidence unit

Each observation below names a PDF-viewer page. The links target free online copies supplied by authors, publishers, or their institutions.

Inference unit

“Use in the agent notes” is my editorial inference from the sampled style. It is not a claim that the source authors prescribe an agent curriculum.

Selection rule

Borrow teaching methods, not distinctive prose or page design. Prefer a synthesis suited to advanced graduates who have used agents but have not built them.

Editorial conclusion

No single reference has the right mix by itself. The most useful combination is the problem-to-model progression of Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, Bishop's and Murphy's interpreted examples, Bach's explicit assumptions and limitations, Mohri's proof structure, and the operational specificity of Mitchell, ISLP, and Phuong–Hutter. The prose should be more expansive than the most compressed theory references.

Nine transferable elements

  1. Start with a consequential problem, then formalize it

    Several books begin with an intelligible task before introducing notation. The papaya classification problem becomes a formal learning model; a diagnostic test becomes a Bayesian calculation. Agent chapters can begin with a concrete failure or decision—such as a tool timeout after a possible purchase—and then derive the state, observation, authority, and verification machinery needed to reason about it.

    Seen in Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, p. 33, Murphy I, p. 76, ISLP, p. 12, and Hardt & Recht, p. 11.

  2. Build each chapter around a recurring conceptual spine

    A unifying lens lets a reader connect later results to earlier ones. Murphy uses probabilistic modeling; Bach organizes learning theory around estimation, approximation, and optimization; Hardt and Recht move from prediction to action. The agent notes should repeatedly return to a run's state, policy, observations, resources, authority, and outcome. New mechanisms should change a specified part of that model rather than appear as unrelated product features.

    Seen in Murphy I, p. 31, Bach, p. 11, and Hardt & Recht, p. 21.

  3. Define objects, notation, and assumptions before using them

    A notation guide helps, but the first local use still needs an explanation. State what each variable ranges over, what an operator means, and which assumptions make a claim true. For agent systems, distinguish an actual environment state from an observation, a model proposal from an executed action, and a successful tool response from a verified outcome. An algorithm should state its inputs, outputs, parameters, and permitted effects.

    Seen in Bishop, p. 11, Murphy I, p. 803, Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, p. 43, and Phuong–Hutter, p. 16.

  4. Derive results in visible steps, then interpret the result

    The theory-oriented books name a proposition or guarantee, state conditions, derive it, and explain what the bound means. Murphy's optimization treatment also turns a mathematical monotonicity result into a debugging check. Agent mathematics should do the same: show where a reliability product, a context-cost term, or a permission invariant comes from, then state what the equation predicts and what it does not establish.

    Seen in Mohri et al., p. 33, Bach, p. 239, Murphy I, p. 342, and Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, p. 392.

  5. Use a running example and figures that carry an argument

    Bishop develops several concepts through polynomial curve fitting, and Murphy works Bayes' rule through a diagnostic-test example. ESL compares least squares with nearest neighbors before moving to more complex methods. A useful agent figure should similarly make a state transition, trust boundary, dependency, or cost trade-off visible. Its caption should explain what the reader is meant to infer; it should not merely restate labels.

    Seen in Bishop, p. 24, Murphy I, p. 76, ESL, p. 30, and ISLP, p. 27.

  6. Make algorithms operational without losing the model

    Mitchell pairs theoretical questions with traces of algorithms. ISLP follows concepts with labs that show code and output. The transformer paper specifies pseudocode with inputs, outputs, parameters, and dimensions. For agent development, include short executable examples of a tool call, context builder, retry state machine, or verifier. Explain how each line implements the preceding model, and keep a consequential operation behind an explicit permission check.

    Seen in Mitchell, p. 4, ISLP, p. 125, and Phuong–Hutter, p. 6.

  7. Compare mechanisms by assumptions, evidence, and failure mode

    A method comparison is stronger when it explains why a method succeeds or fails in a particular setting. ESL contrasts the structural assumptions of least squares and nearest neighbors; Hardt and Recht distinguish empirical observations from explanations; Murphy's advanced volume ends one causal-estimation section with practical advice. For agents, compare a prompt rule, a tool gate, a sandbox, and a verifier by what each can enforce. Report task success, cost, delay, and risk as separate outcomes.

    Seen in ESL, p. 30, Hardt & Recht, p. 116, and Murphy II, p. 1241.

  8. State the boundary of a guarantee and where knowledge is missing

    Formal statements gain teaching value when their idealizations are visible. Bach discusses when asymptotic analysis may mislead at finite sample sizes. Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David identify the conditions and proof gap for a tighter bound. Telgarsky explicitly marks the weaknesses and omitted topics of his chosen perspective. Agent chapters should distinguish a model property, an engineering check, an observed benchmark result, and an unverified product claim.

    Seen in Bach, p. 123, Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, p. 392, and Telgarsky, p. 3.

  9. Use exercises and prose pacing to consolidate the argument

    Exercises can ask for a derivation, an assumption check, a counterexample, and a practical design decision rather than only arithmetic. ISLP separates conceptual questions from applied work; Mohri connects exercises to chapter notes; Mitchell asks readers to trace an algorithm on altered data. In the main prose, give one claim room to breathe. State a result, explain the mechanism, and then discuss its limitation in separate sentences when those are distinct ideas.

    Seen in ISLP, p. 73, Mohri et al., p. 40, Mitchell, p. 60, and Murphy I, p. 76.

What is distinctive in each reference

The portraits identify usable habits and a boundary for borrowing them. They describe sampled pages, not every chapter.

Theory textbook

Foundations of Machine Learning

Mehryar Mohri, Afshin Rostamizadeh, Ameet Talwalkar

Edition / version: Second edition (MIT Press, 2018). Source page

Begins a formal topic with questions, defines a learning model, gives theorem–proof–example sequences, and interprets sample-complexity bounds. Chapter notes and exercises connect the result to literature and extensions. Its deliberately succinct proofs are useful as a structural model; the agent notes should explain more of the intermediate reasoning.

Preface p. 14 · PAC setup p. 26 · Result and example p. 33

Concept-and-figure textbook

Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning

Christopher M. Bishop

Edition / version: 2006 Springer volume; no numbered edition is stated in this PDF. Source page

Introduces a running example before broadening to probability and decision theory. Figures and captions establish what variables mean before equations become demanding. A dedicated notation section makes conventions explicit. Bishop states that conveying concepts takes priority over maximal mathematical rigor; combine his pacing and visuals with fuller proofs from the theory texts.

Notation p. 11 · Curve fit p. 24 · Probability example p. 32

Unifying reference

Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction

Kevin P. Murphy

Edition / version: MIT Press, 2022. This online PDF is dated 18 April 2025; no numbered edition is stated. Source page

Uses a probabilistic lens across topics, restates an equation in words, then tests it on a concrete numerical case. Later derivations connect formal properties to practical diagnostics. Examples, figures, code-generated plots, cross-references, and a notation appendix make a large reference navigable. For agent notes, the most valuable habit is the prose interpretation after each formal result.

Framing p. 31 · Bayes example p. 76 · Derivation p. 342

Advanced reference

Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics

Kevin P. Murphy

Edition / version: MIT Press, 2023. This online PDF is dated 10 December 2025; no numbered edition is stated. Source page

Defines advanced objects systematically, often with a small example before a more general model. Causal graphs and optimization figures accompany derivations; selected sections close with practical advice. Use this as a depth and cross-reference model for topics such as partial observability or causal evaluation, while keeping local definitions in each chapter.

Probability setup p. 39 · Causal example p. 247 · Practical advice p. 1241

Statistical methods textbook

The Elements of Statistical Learning

Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, Jerome Friedman

Edition / version: Second edition (2009), corrected 12th printing of 13 January 2017. Source page

Defines a prediction problem through scientific examples, then compares methods by the assumptions they impose and the errors they incur. Data examples and exercises make the contrast concrete. Its technical prose can be dense. Borrow the comparative method and empirical figures, but unpack the bridges between assumptions and conclusions for this audience.

Applications p. 20 · Method contrast p. 30 · Exercises p. 58

Applied textbook

An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python

Gareth James, Daniela Witten, Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, Jonathan Taylor

Edition / version: Python edition; first printing dated 5 July 2023. Source page

Starts with recognizable data sets and visible patterns, explains concepts before equations, then gives labs with executable Python and displayed output. Exercises distinguish conceptual understanding from application. Its accessible cadence is valuable for agent-development labs; the mathematical depth of the agent notes should remain above its introductory level.

Data-first opening p. 12 · Figure interpretation p. 27 · Lab p. 125

First-principles theory

Learning Theory from First Principles

Francis Bach

Edition / version: Author PDF dated 26 May 2025; no numbered edition is stated. Source page

Starts by asking why the theory matters, provides chapter summaries and prerequisites, and carries assumptions through detailed derivations. It discusses what asymptotic results cannot tell us about finite problems. Adopt its explicit assumption ledger and limiting-case discussion. Add worked agent traces when a proof would otherwise float free of the system being modeled.

Motivation p. 11 · Chapter map p. 19 · Limitation p. 123

Algorithms-and-practice textbook

Machine Learning

Tom M. Mitchell

Edition / version: McGraw-Hill, 1997; no numbered edition is stated. Source page

Frames machine learning through problems, algorithms, assumptions, and the balance of theory with practice. Definitions and traces are developed on concrete tasks, while exercises vary the task or order of examples. Borrow the progressive algorithm trace. Do not import dated examples or treat historical implementation details as current practice.

Preface p. 3 · Formalization p. 35 · Trace exercise p. 60

Historical-conceptual monograph

Patterns, Predictions, and Actions

Moritz Hardt, Benjamin Recht

Edition / version: 2022 print book; online preprint compiled 8 June 2026. No numbered online edition is stated. Source page

Uses a historical episode to establish why a prediction problem matters, then develops formal criteria and follows their consequences for decisions. Later empirical examples are set beside unresolved theoretical explanations. Borrow the connection from mechanism to social or operational consequence. Use historical framing sparingly when it would delay an agent chapter's main argument.

Historical opening p. 11 · Formal criterion p. 33 · Evidence boundary p. 116

Formal learning textbook

Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms

Shai Shalev-Shwartz, Shai Ben-David

Edition / version: Cambridge, 2014 author PDF; no numbered edition is stated. Its pagination differs from print. Source page

Moves from a concrete classification problem to a formal model, precise quantifiers, theorem statements, and proofs. The reader can see exactly which assumptions a guarantee needs. When the strongest proof needs more machinery, the text identifies the looser result it actually proves. This is the clearest model for formal agent claims, provided every symbol receives local explanation.

Motivating task p. 33 · Definition p. 43 · Proof boundary p. 392

Theory lecture notes

Deep Learning Theory Lecture Notes

Matus Telgarsky

Edition / version: Version 2021-10-27 v0.0-e7150f2d (alpha); lecture notes, not a numbered textbook edition. Source page

Names its chosen theoretical perspective, its weaknesses, and topics it omits. Short propositions and remarks show a route to simplified proofs. The sampled version also contains draft markers and unfinished sections. Use the honesty about scope and the proof simplification; do not imitate the shorthand or unfinished editorial state.

Scope and weaknesses p. 3 · Proposition p. 15 · Draft marker p. 63

Short technical paper · not a textbook

Formal Algorithms for Transformers

Mary Phuong, Marcus Hutter

Edition / version: arXiv version 1, dated 19 July 2022; a paper, not a textbook edition. Source page

Responds to vague architectural descriptions with formal pseudocode. Algorithms state inputs, outputs, parameters, and dimensions; a notation appendix collects symbols. This is a strong model for a tool or harness contract. As a 16-page paper, it moves too quickly to supply the narrative and exercises needed in graduate lecture notes.

A house style for the agent lecture notes

The following sequence preserves the transcript as the topic spine without making the prose a recount of the class. Video timestamps can remain in section headings; the chapter body should read as an independent textbook chapter.

  1. Open with a task and its consequence. Describe a specific agent decision or failure in a few paragraphs. Identify what the user wants and what an incorrect action would change.
  2. Define the objects of study. Introduce the environment state, observations, history, available actions, permission state, and outcome measure. Define each technical term and symbol before its first substantive use.
  3. State the assumptions and claim. Separate a formal guarantee from a heuristic design rule and from a product-specific observation. Say which source or experiment supports an empirical claim.
  4. Derive the mechanism. Give intermediate equations or transition steps. Explain what each term contributes. Include a limiting case, counterexample, or numerical check where it clarifies the result.
  5. Work the original task through the model. Show a short trace, code fragment, diagram, or table. Inspect both a successful path and a path involving uncertainty, stale information, or partial failure.
  6. Compare alternatives. State what changes when the tool surface, context policy, memory store, or verifier changes. Compare success, cost, delay, and risk separately rather than hiding them in one score.
  7. Close with limits and exercises. Identify what the model leaves out, which facts remain unsettled, and what evidence would test the proposed design. Include derivation, diagnosis, and design exercises.
Example of the preferred prose rhythm

Compressed: “Evaluation should report the vector (S, C, D, R), not collapse everything into a single leaderboard rank.”

Expanded: “Let S measure task success, C resource cost, D delay, and R risk. Report these four quantities separately. A system that improves success by doubling cost may be desirable in one application and unacceptable in another. A single rank hides the information needed to make that decision.”

Limits and cautions

TemptationEditorial decision
Copy the shortest proof styleKeep theorem structure and precision, but show intermediate steps and an interpretation. Mohri explicitly favors succinctness; your stated preference favors a little more room.
Copy an introductory voice wholesaleUse ISLP's examples and labs while retaining graduate-level models, quantifiers, and failure analysis.
Let code replace explanationUse Phuong–Hutter-style contracts and executable snippets after the model is introduced. Explain what the code guarantees and what it only attempts.
Use theory as an unqualified product claimMark assumptions and evidence. A theorem about an idealized policy, a benchmark result, and a provider's implementation documentation answer different questions.
Imitate distinctive wording or page designBorrow pedagogical structure, not source prose, diagrams, or typography. The report paraphrases the references and links to freely accessible online editions for inspection.
